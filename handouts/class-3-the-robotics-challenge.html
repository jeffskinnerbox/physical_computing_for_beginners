Defining physical computing

Two ways to define Physical Computing

General technical definition

The combination of software and hardware to create interactive systems capable of communicating with the physical environment using sensors, microcontrollers, and actuators.

Educational definition

Taking code off a flat computer screen and bringing it into the real world — making abstract programming concepts tangible through lights, sounds, and motion.

Physical computing is the broader conceptual umbrella; robotics shares its technological foundation but aims at different goals.

Defining robots

Sense. Think. Act. All three, or it's not a robot.

A robot senses its environment, processes that information, and executes complex physical actions — autonomously or semi-autonomously.

Sense

Cameras, microphones, sonar, or touch sensors gather information about the physical world.

Think

An onboard computer or microcontroller processes the data, applies instructions or AI, and decides.

Act

Motors, hydraulic arms, wheels, or speakers move, manipulate, or interact — with no real-time human control.

Our challenge — sensing

Can we measure — directly or indirectly — everything we need to make good decisions?

Sensing is the foundation every other decision depends on: think and act can only be as good as what the robot is able to sense.

Our challenge — motors

Identical motors don't behave identically

Not created equal

Identical brand and model electric motors do not perform the same way.

Physical wear

Electric motors are physical objects that have uneven friction.

Power sag

When used, battery voltages and currents will sag.

Our challenge — wheels

Wheels slip, jam, and even slide sideways

Slip, jam, slide

Under real-world conditions, wheels will slip, jam, or even slide sideways.

Deformation

If the wheel deforms, it introduces errors that are hard to measure or predict.

Our challenge — navigation

Knowing where you are is as hard as getting there

The mission: leave the room

1. Identify the door — how?

2. Maneuver to the door — what path?

3. Identify an obstacle — what counts as one?

4. Avoid the obstacle — what path?

Which way is forward?

A sudden slip, motor sag, or wheel jam can silently redirect the robot's heading.

Without a way to check orientation against the world, "forward" can quietly become a new direction — and the robot won't know it happened.

The throughline

Robotics requires engineering reliable behavior from unreliable parts and processes

  • Sense — measurement is never perfect or complete.
  • Motors — identical hardware still behaves differently.
  • Wheels — slip, jam, and deform in ways hard to predict.
  • Navigation — orientation can change without warning.
